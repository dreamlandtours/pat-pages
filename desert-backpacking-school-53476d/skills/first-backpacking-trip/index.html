What actually happens on a first backpacking trip?

Walking into Coyote Gulch

You carry about 35 pounds for 5 to 8 miles a day, wear the same hiking outfit every day, drink treated water from rivers and springs, sleep in a small tent and go to the bathroom without an outhouse. None of it's hard once someone has shown you how. The real test isn't one long day. It's doing a moderate day again tomorrow, on tired legs, after a night on the ground.

Every year we take people on multi-day trips who are carrying a loaded backpack for the first time. Almost all of them arrive with the same private worry, and it's almost never about the miles. It's about the bathroom, about being the slowest person in the group, or about not knowing how a pack is supposed to sit. This page hands over that invisible knowledge in one place. The full version, with every detail, is our long guide: Your First Backpacking Trip: What Actually Happens Out There.

Am I ready?

Backpacking isn't hiking with a heavier pack. It's hiking with a heavier pack and then doing it again. The limit for most first-timers isn't lungs or strength. It's repeatability. Ask yourself three things.

  1. Can you walk 5 to 8 miles carrying 35 pounds over uneven ground? Trail, sand, rock and roots, not pavement.
  2. Can you get up the next morning and do it again, and then a third day?
  3. Can you be on your feet outdoors for 6 to 8 hours a day, including some scrambling and river crossings?

Three confident yeses mean you're ready for an easier route. A "probably, with training" means you have work to do and time to do it. If any answer is a clear no right now, choose an easier trip or a later date.

How do I train?

Give yourself at least eight weeks. If you have less, start anyway. The most valuable thing you can do is walk with weight on your back. Cardio machines build your engine, but they don't teach your shoulders, hips, feet and lower back what six hours under 35 pounds feels like.

  • Use water jugs as weight. A gallon weighs just over 8 pounds, so four gallons make a full 35-pound load. If you get in over your head, pour them out and walk home light.
  • Train downhill on purpose. First-timers underestimate descents. Going down is where quads get loaded, toes jam into boots and knees complain.
  • Break in your boots. Walk at least 30 to 50 miles in them before the trip, in the socks you'll wear.
  • Sleep on the floor once. Put your sleeping pad on a hard floor for a full night. You'll learn at home whether you need a thicker pad.

How many clothes do I bring?

One hiking outfit and one sleep layer. That's the whole system, for three days or five. You hike in the same shirt and pants every day. Every first-timer packs more than this, and every one of them carries clothes they never touch.

Leave cotton at home. It soaks up sweat, stops insulating the moment it's wet and takes forever to dry. And yes, you'll smell. Everyone does, and by day three nobody notices.

To wash socks and underwear, carry water at least 200 feet from the source, rinse without soap, wring the item inside a bandana and clip it to your pack inside out. In desert air, socks rinsed at breakfast are usually dry by lunch.

How do I carry the pack?

On your hips, not your shoulders. About 80 percent of the weight belongs on your pelvis. If your shoulders burn after an hour, the pack doesn't fit yet. That's a fixable problem.

  1. Loosen every strap before you put the pack on. Lift it onto a knee first, then swing an arm through.
  2. Hip belt first. Center the padding over the top of your hip bones and pull it snug.
  3. Shoulder straps second. Snug, with no gap, but not lifting weight off your hips.
  4. Load lifters third. The small straps above your shoulders, pulled to about a 45-degree angle.
  5. Sternum strap last, with light tension only.

Inside the pack: sleeping bag at the bottom, heavy things (food, water, tent) in the middle against your spine, jackets and first aid on top, snacks and sunscreen in the hip pockets. Every hour or so, shift a little weight between hips and shoulders.

How does going to the bathroom work?

There are no outhouses on most desert backpacking routes. The Grand Canyon's established campgrounds are the exception.

  • Peeing: walk a short distance from camp and the trail, ideally 70 steps from water, and go on rock or sand, not on plants. Pack out toilet paper in a small zip-top bag, or use a pee cloth.
  • Pooping where waste bags are required: in Paria Canyon, Buckskin Gulch and Coyote Gulch all solid human waste must be carried out. A waste bag (often called a WAG bag) is a double-bag kit with a gelling powder that solidifies liquid and controls odor. You use it, seal it and carry it to the trailhead. First-timers dread this and then report that it was fine.
  • Pooping where burial is allowed: walk about 200 feet (roughly 70 adult steps) from camp, trail and any water or wash. Dig a hole 6 to 8 inches deep, use it, fill it in and put a rock on top. Pack out the toilet paper. Never bury or burn it: desert soil is dry and slow, and paper resurfaces.
  • Hand sanitizer every time. The most common way a backpacking trip goes wrong is a stomach bug passed hand to hand.
  • On your period: entirely manageable. Everything gets packed out in an opaque zip-top bag. Bring more supplies than you think you need.

Where does drinking water come from?

From rivers, springs and potholes along the route, and sometimes from water placed in advance. Every water source is treated before you drink, by filter, by chemical treatment or both. Silty water, which is normal in the Paria and the Colorado, is left to settle first and then treated.

  • Carry up to 3 liters of capacity and refill as you go. Water weighs about 2.2 pounds per liter, so you carry only what you need to reach the next source.
  • In desert heat, drink half a liter to a full liter per hour while moving.
  • Use electrolytes every day. Plain water alone, while you sweat heavily, dilutes your sodium.

What do I eat, and how much?

Backpackers burn a lot, often 3,000 to 5,000 calories a day on a demanding route. Don't wait for mealtimes. Eat something small every 60 to 90 minutes while you walk. Bring more variety than volume: something salty, something sweet and something you look forward to. Nuts, jerky, dried mango, salted crackers and gummy candy travel well. Chocolate melts.

What is sleeping out there like?

Your first night is often not great. Your third may be the best sleep you have had in months. Desert nights are colder than almost everyone expects, in every season.

  • Your sleeping pad matters as much as your bag. Cold comes up from the ground.
  • Sleep in a dry layer and dry socks.
  • Eat a small snack before bed, and pee before you get in.
  • The night has a soundtrack: wind, distant rockfall, mice and coyotes. None of it is a threat. Ear plugs help light sleepers.

What are the real risks?

Not the ones the movies suggest. Heat and dehydration come first. Falls on uneven ground come second. Flash floods matter enormously in slot canyons. Rattlesnakes want nothing to do with you: watch where you put hands and feet. Scorpions are a minor nuisance: shake out your shoes. Bears aren't a factor on our routes.

In wilderness terrain there's usually no quick exit. Emergency evacuation in canyon country almost always means a helicopter, and helicopters are for true medical emergencies. For everything else, the way out is walking to the trailhead under your own power. That's why the three questions at the top matter.

Which trip should come first?

Pick a route with moderate daily miles and little elevation change. Of the trips we guide, two welcome motivated, fit first-timers.
