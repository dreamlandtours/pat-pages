How hot does it really get inside a desert canyon?

Below the rim in the Grand Canyon

Much hotter than the forecast says. A forecast temperature is measured in the shade, about five feet off the ground, with air moving freely around the sensor. On a 115°F day in a sun-exposed canyon, the heat that reaches your body from the sun above and the hot rock on every side works out to roughly 140 to 150°F. In high summer that means walking at dawn and being out of the sun by late morning, or not going at all.

What does the forecast number measure?

Every official temperature is taken in the shade, about five feet above the ground, with air moving freely around the sensor. That's the international standard, and it's the right way to compare one day with the next. It leaves out the two things that matter most in a canyon: direct sunlight, and the heat coming off the rock around you.

A forecast of 115°F is a shade temperature. Nobody hikes in that environment. No five-foot pocket of ventilated shade follows you down a canyon.

Why is the "feels like" number on my phone wrong for the desert?

The "feels like" number measures humidity, not sunlight. It was built for muggy places, where damp air keeps sweat from evaporating. In desert air with humidity around 10 percent, that math comes out at or below the real temperature. On a 115-degree day your phone may report "feels like 110°F".

In canyon country humidity isn't the danger. Radiation is, and the "feels like" number doesn't measure radiation at all.

What do heat-safety professionals measure instead?

The military, OSHA and sports medicine use the Wet Bulb Globe Temperature, or WBGT. The instrument includes a matte-black globe that absorbs sunlight and radiant heat the way a human body does.

  • The military suspends all strenuous outdoor training at a WBGT of 90°F. They call it black flag.
  • On a 115°F day in full canyon sun, the WBGT works out in the low to mid 90s. That's past the point where the Army tells soldiers to stop.
  • A related measure, mean radiant temperature, adds up the heat striking your body from every surface: the sun above and hot rock on every side. In a canyon at 115°F air temperature it comes to roughly 140 to 150°F.

Fitness doesn't change that physics. It applies to marathon runners and first-time hikers alike.

Why are canyons the worst case?

Four things stack on top of the forecast number down in a canyon.

FactorWhat it does
Dark rockDesert rock in full sun commonly runs 40 to 60°F hotter than the air. On a 115°F day that means surfaces near 170°F, hot enough to burn skin on contact. The darker the rock, the worse it gets.
Walls on both sidesA narrow canyon radiates stored heat at you from every direction and reflects extra sunlight down on top of you. The shape works like an oven.
The hot ground layerAir near ground level in direct sun runs 15 to 25°F hotter than the official reading. Scrambling with your hands on the rock puts you inside that layer.
No windMoving air is your body's main cooling system in dry heat. Deep canyons block it, so your sweat evaporates into a still, hot layer of air.

How do I plan a hot day in canyon country?

In high summer, the honest answer to "should we do this hike at midday?" is often no.

Not with us, and not with anybody. You don't need a guide to be safe out here in summer. You need a plan.

  1. Start at dawn. Avoid long hikes between 10am and 4pm in the hot months, and be out of the sun by late morning. The hot months here are June, July and August, and some days in May and September.
  2. Drink on a schedule. Plan on half a liter to a full liter of water per hour while you move, and the full liter in high summer. For a day in the heat, budget at least a gallon per person. Drink before you're thirsty.
  3. Add electrolytes every day. Plain water all day while you sweat heavily dilutes your sodium. The result is headache, nausea and lethargy, which people then mistake for needing more water. Use electrolyte tablets or powder and eat salty snacks.
  4. Check your urine. Pale straw is the target. Dark means drink more. Clear all day means you may need more electrolytes and less plain water.
  5. Cover up. A light-colored, long-sleeve sun shirt is cooler than bare arms in dry heat. Add a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen at every break. Sunburn makes your body worse at shedding heat on the days that follow.
  6. Rest in shade at midday. On an all-day hike, sit out the middle of the day under a cliff or a tree.
  7. Turn around while it still feels too early.

Two more tools worth knowing: a sun umbrella, rigged to your pack straps so your hands stay free, and, on a hot Grand Canyon trip, a cotton sun shirt worn damp on purpose. Leave the dog at home in summer.

What does Dreamland do differently in summer?

  • Summer day trips leave at sunrise. Our Wave tours start at 6am or earlier in the hot months.
  • Guides carry cooling cloths and frozen water bottles for guests, and more water than looks reasonable in the photos.
  • Guides watch for the early signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke the whole day.
  • We've pulled guests off an itinerary they were excited about because the rock had turned into a griddle. Nobody thanks us in the moment. They tend to thank us at dinner.

Which heat illnesses should I know before I go?

Hard effort in desert temperatures can bring on dehydration, hyponatremia (sodium diluted by too much plain water), heat exhaustion or heat stroke. Learn to recognize each one and what first aid it needs before a summer hike. A Wilderness First Aid course teaches exactly that.
